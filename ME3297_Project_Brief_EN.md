# **1\. What this project is**

Each team takes one **real, public supply-chain dataset**, frames one **operational decision** that the data could support, and then builds, compares and interprets machine-learning models that inform that decision.

The output is not a model with a high score. It is a defensible recommendation to a supply-chain manager, supported by evidence and honest about its limits. A team that reports 0.94 accuracy and says nothing about what the company should do has not finished the project.

The project is worth **30% of the course mark** and is budgeted at **45 hours per student**, which is the major-assignment allocation in the course syllabus.

**Real data only.** Simulated data, data generated by an LLM, and textbook toy sets do not satisfy this assignment, however realistic they look. Real data is messy on purpose: dealing with that mess is most of what is being assessed.

# **4\. Define the decision, not the dataset**

A project is defined by a question that a manager could act on tomorrow morning.

|              | **Too weak**                            | **Strong enough**                                                                                                          |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question** | "Analyse the Olist e-commerce dataset." | "Which orders should be flagged at dispatch as at risk of late delivery, so customer service can contact the buyer first?" |
| **Target**   | "Predict something useful."             | "Delivered later than the promised date — a binary label known 10–20 days after dispatch."                                 |
| **Value**    | "Improve accuracy."                     | "At what alert rate does the cost of checking an order exceed the value of catching a late one?"                           |

Every proposal must therefore state four things:

1. the **decision**, the **decision-maker**, and the **moment** at which the decision is taken;
2. what the model predicts, and what is known **at that moment** — this is the data-leakage test, and it is where most projects go wrong;
3. the **baseline** the model must beat: current practice, a simple rule, or the majority class;
4. what a wrong prediction costs **in each direction** — a false alarm and a missed case are rarely equally expensive in a supply chain.

## **Route B — propose your own**

- **Real and observational** — recorded from actual operations, not simulated, not generated.
- **Accessible** — publicly downloadable, or company data with written permission to use it in coursework. Confidential company data must be anonymised before it leaves the company, and the report must say what was removed.
- **Large enough** — at least **1,000 rows and 8 columns**, with at least one usable target variable or a genuine unsupervised structure to find.

- **Supply-chain relevant** — sourcing, production, inventory, transport, warehousing, distribution, demand, returns, or service level.

**Vietnamese and company data are welcome under Route B** and often make the strongest projects, because the recommendation lands on a real desk. Start early: getting permission and cleaning an export takes longer than students expect.

# **6\. Technical requirements**

Every project must do all of the following. Items marked with a bullet in §11 are where marks are most often lost.

1. **Document the data.** Row and column counts, a data dictionary for every column actually used, missing-value and outlier counts, and the target distribution or class balance.
2. **Prepare the data reproducibly.** Every cleaning, imputation, encoding and scaling decision is stated and justified. All fitted preprocessing sits inside a scikit-learn Pipeline / ColumnTransformer.
3. **Split before fitting.** A train/test split, or a time-based split for any dataset with a date. Nothing derived from the test set may enter training. Name at least one leakage risk specific to your dataset and show how it is avoided.
4. **Compare at least three model families,** of which at least two are taught in Weeks 6–13 (decision trees, artificial neural networks, support vector machines, clustering, association rules, Bayesian networks). The baseline does not count as one of the three.
5. **Validate properly.** k-fold cross-validation on the training set, or rolling-origin validation for time series. Report the **spread** of the metric across folds, not only its mean.
6. **Report metrics that match the decision.** Accuracy alone is not acceptable for an imbalanced problem. Justify the chosen metric against §4.
7. **Interpret the model.** Which features drive it, and does that agree with supply-chain reasoning? Use feature importances, coefficients, tree structure, or partial-dependence plots — whichever the chosen model supports.
8. **Turn the model into a recommendation,** with an order-of-magnitude estimate of the operational effect: orders flagged per week, stock-out days avoided, freight cost saved, hours of labour redirected. An approximate figure with stated assumptions is expected; no figure at all is not.
9. **State the limits.** What the model cannot do, where the data is unrepresentative of the situation you are advising on, and what would have to be true for the recommendation to hold.

## **If your main method is unsupervised**

Teams whose central method is clustering or association-rule mining replace requirements 4–6 with: at least two clustering algorithms, or two clearly different support/confidence regimes, compared; a stated and defended method for choosing k or the thresholds; and a stability check — does the solution survive resampling or a change of random seed? Business interpretation of the segments or rules carries the same weight as the algorithm itself.

## **Notes on individual sets**

- **No. 2 (Olist)** requires joining nine tables before any modelling can start. It is the most realistic and the most work — a good choice for a confident team.
- **No. 3 (health commodities)** is a genuine public-health supply chain. It suits teams interested in hospital, pharmaceutical or humanitarian logistics.
- **No. 4 (Online Retail II)** needs real cleaning: cancellations, negative quantities and missing customer IDs are all present and all matter.
- **No. 5 (Rossmann)** must be split by time, never shuffled. Shuffling a time series is a leakage error and is marked as one.
- **No. 6 (pharmacy sales)** has few columns, so most of the work is feature engineering — calendar effects, lags, rolling means.
- **No. 7 (garment factory)** is the smallest set here and the easiest to overfit. It can score very well, but only with careful validation and modest claims.
- **No. 8 (Instacart)** is large. Plan to work on a documented random sample of customers rather than the full file.

# **11\. What loses marks**

These are the failures that appear most often, in roughly the order they appear. Reading this list before submitting is worth more than another hour of tuning.

- Accuracy reported on an imbalanced dataset with no other metric beside it.
- A scaler or an imputer fitted on the whole dataset before the split.
- A feature that would not be known at the moment the decision is made — the actual delivery date used to predict whether delivery will be late is the classic case.
- Model comparison with no baseline, so the reader cannot tell whether any of it helped.
- A single number from a single split, presented as if it were exact.
- Charts with no axis labels, no units, or no caption.
- A recommendation that does not follow from the results — or results with no recommendation at all.
- Code that does not run from a clean start, or that produces different numbers from the report.
- A report that describes everything that was done and never says what the company should do.
- A conclusion stated with more confidence than 1,000 rows of data can carry.

The marking rubric is a separate document. Read it before writing the proposal, not after the report — the criteria are the same at every checkpoint, and they say what "good" means in more detail than this brief does.
